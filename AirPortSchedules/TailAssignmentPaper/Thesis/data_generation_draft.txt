2.1 The procedure
Khaled et al. derive their test set from a single real timetable of 1,580 flight legs spanning 30 days across 37 airports. The procedure has three steps. A minimum-flow computation over the connection network first determines the smallest fleet that can cover the timetable, returning with it a partition of the legs into per-aircraft flight sets. A subset of np aircraft is then drawn at random from that partition. Finally a further subset of δ × np aircraft is drawn, and the leg set of the instance is the union of their flight sets.
The parameter δ, which the authors call density, is the ratio between the minimum number of aircraft needed to cover the timetable and the number actually available. At δ = 1 the fleet is exactly the minimum required; below 1 there is slack and more freedom to find a feasible answer. The published experiments span δ in {0.95, 1}, fleets of 10, 20, 30 and 40 aircraft, and horizons of 7, 15, 21 and 30 days, giving 400 instances at ten per configuration.
2.2 What the design guarantees
Two properties follow directly from the construction. Feasibility is structural: because the leg set is the union of complete rotations drawn from a feasible cover, a schedule operating exactly those legs with exactly those aircraft is known to exist before the instance is written out. Realism is inherited: block times, station geography, turn times and cost data all come from operational records rather than from a fitted distribution, which no synthetic generator can fully reproduce.
Density is a real and well-chosen difficulty dial, and the authors were the first to study its influence on this problem class systematically. Their timing results show computational effort rising monotonically with it: on the largest size class average solution time rises from 956.73 seconds at δ = 0.9 to 1,195.68 seconds at δ = 1.
2.3 What the design leaves uncontrolled
The behaviour the authors report on their own instances is summarised below.
Reported indicator Value across the published test set
Integrality gap at the root node 0.25% on average, below 0.5% on every instance
Integral variables in the relaxation optimum between 86.9% and 98.6%
Branch-and-bound nodes to optimality fewer than 15 on average, 55 at maximum
Residual gap after search 0.00% on all but one configuration
The authors read these figures, correctly, as evidence that their formulation is strong: a relaxation landing within half a percent of the integer optimum, with a search that closes in fewer than fifteen nodes, is a formulation doing almost all of the work. Read instead as a statement about the instances, the same figures say something different — that these instances are resolved at or very near the root node, and therefore cannot separate a good search strategy from a poor one. A benchmark on which every method finishes in fifteen nodes ranks nothing.
Three further properties follow from the sub-sampling design rather than from any oversight in it. Because every instance is a union of rotations lifted from one real timetable, the routing structure available to a solver is whatever that timetable happened to contain, and there is no parameter that varies it. Because the legs are drawn as complete rotations, the sampled schedule is always a feasible answer, but nothing characterises how many other feasible answers exist or how far they differ from it. And because the optimum is whatever the solver reports, there is no independent ground truth against which a solver implementation can be checked — a circularity that becomes material as soon as the question shifts from how fast a model solves to whether it is solving the right problem.
On maintenance the authors are explicit. A checks fall due roughly every 65 flight-hours, B checks every 300 to 600 flight-hours, and C and D checks once every one to four years; they conclude that the heavier checks “need not be taken into account in maintenance scheduling” over horizons of a few weeks, and model type A only. For a 30-day planning horizon that is sound operational reasoning. Its consequence for a benchmark is that one of the four check types is exercised, and that the cascading reset by which a heavier check subsumes the lighter ones is never triggered at all.

Five techniques do the substantive work. Each was chosen so that feasibility survives its application, either because the construction cannot violate it or because a violation is detected and discarded before it can take effect.
4.1 Eulerian decomposition of the daily flight network
A directed multigraph admits a closed walk traversing every arc exactly once, on each connected component, precisely when every node has equal in-degree and out-degree. That is Euler's theorem, and Hierholzer's algorithm constructs the walks in time linear in the number of arcs. The daily flight network is built to satisfy the balance condition rather than tested for it afterwards.
Aircraft are partitioned into small groups. For each group a closed cycle is laid through the bases of its members, and the cycle is then cut at those base visits into one open walk per aircraft: aircraft j flies from its own base through a shared hub to the base of aircraft j+1, and the last member of the group closes the cycle. Concatenating the walks reproduces the cycle exactly, so every intermediate station contributes one arrival and one departure, and every base is left once and entered once.
Restated at fleet level, the balance condition says that the multiset of overnight positions is preserved from one day to the next. That is what makes repositioning possible without anything breaking: aircraft exchange bases overnight, but the fleet's distribution across those bases is invariant. Two further constraints keep the construction sound. Overnight positions are always maintenance-capable stations, since an aircraft's new base is another aircraft's old one, so a check is always schedulable where the aircraft actually is. And a cycle is emitted whole or not at all, because a partially emitted cycle would leave a station with more departures than arrivals and destroy the very balance the decomposition rests on.
The property is verified rather than assumed. Balance and decomposability are recomputed from the published flight list, not read back from the generator's own bookkeeping, and the Hierholzer routine returns nothing at all on an unbalanced graph rather than silently producing a partial answer. Across all thirty-six combinations of preset, topology and threshold mode, every day is balanced with a maximum node imbalance of zero, and every day decomposes.
The effect is structural rather than cosmetic. On the medium preset the connection density — the number of time-feasible connection arcs per leg — rises from 0.50 in the baseline configuration to 8.53, and the number of distinct overnight stations per aircraft rises from one to nine. In the baseline every aircraft flies out and back from a fixed base, so its route is a star centred on that base and two aircraft are never on the ground together anywhere else. Under the decomposition they meet routinely, which is the precondition for any exchange between them being possible at all.
4.2 Pairwise coprime maintenance periods
The legacy check limits make two of the four types unreachable: C is 600 elapsed days and D is 3,000, against horizons that never exceed 180. This mirrors the modelling decision taken in the reference paper and, for a schedule of a few weeks, it is defensible. For a benchmark it means the interaction between check types — in particular the cascade by which a heavier check resets the lighter ones — is never exercised, and a solver that handles that cascade incorrectly is never caught.
The generator can instead derive all four limits from pairwise coprime periods, each drawn from the prime nearest one of h/8, h/5, h/3.2 and h/2, where h is the horizon in days. Two properties follow. Distinct primes are pairwise coprime, so the least common multiple of any two periods exceeds the horizon: no two check types ever settle into a shared rhythm, and the pattern of resets stays aperiodic across the whole dataset. And because every period is shorter than the horizon, every check type necessarily fires. A and B are counted in flight minutes rather than elapsed days, so their periods are converted through the fleet's estimated daily block time, while C and D are used directly.
Frequent heavy checks make station capacity bind, and that in turn forces a change in how checks are scheduled. Deferring each check to its last legal day piles the fleet onto the same station-days and overruns capacity. Performing a check early is always legal, so a check is instead pulled forward into a free slot within a lead window. That window must be capped at half the check's own cycle: a lead longer than the cycle makes a check permanently due, so it is repeated the day after every reset, the aircraft never flies again, and the horizon yields no legs whatsoever. Short coprime periods reach that failure immediately, which is how the cap came to be needed.
On the medium preset the number of active check types rises from two to four, the number of maintenance events from 23 to 193, and peak station-day capacity utilisation from 0.33 to 1.00. The last of these is the substantive change: station capacity stops being slack and starts being a constraint that a solver has to reason about.
4.3 A feasibility-preserving Metropolis walk
Given one feasible schedule, the space of feasible schedules can be explored by a Markov chain whose moves preserve feasibility by construction. The move used here is a segment exchange: two aircraft that are both on the ground at a common station swap a bounded run of activities between them.
Feasibility is an invariant of the chain rather than a term in its objective, and the distinction matters. Splicing at a shared station preserves station-to-station continuity structurally; the multiset of maintenance events is unchanged by an exchange, so station capacity occupancy is invariant by construction; and turn times and maintenance threshold compliance are re-checked on both rebuilt routes before a proposal is scored at all. A proposal that would break feasibility is discarded rather than penalised, so the chain cannot leave the feasible region no matter how the objective is weighted.
The move is its own inverse, since exchanging the same two segments back restores the previous state, which keeps the proposal distribution symmetric and the Metropolis acceptance rule valid. Energy is the negation of a weighted complexity score when structural diversity is wanted, or the schedule cost when a cheap incumbent is wanted, in both cases under geometric cooling.
In practice the chain reassigns 48% of legs away from the planted schedule while every accepted state certifies as feasible. Its value is twofold. It demonstrates constructively that an instance admits many genuinely different feasible answers, which no sub-sampled instance can report about itself. And it produces those answers as a ranked pool, which is what makes the solver validation protocol of Section 7 possible.
4.4 Correlated cost structure
Cost enters feasibility nowhere, so the cost model can be varied freely without any risk to the guarantees established above. Independent uniform noise applied to a base cost — the original model — leaves the columns of the cost matrix uncorrelated, which is broadly the condition under which a linear relaxation tends to be near-integral. The generator offers instead a low-rank Gaussian factor structure combining an aircraft effect, a station effect and an aircraft-station interaction at a configurable correlation, with a tunable share of legs collapsed into a near-tie band.
The measurable effect is decorrelation between the cost signal and the planted answer. On the medium preset the share of legs whose planted aircraft is also the cheapest available falls from 0.25 to 0.14, so a solver can no longer approach the optimum simply by reading off each leg's cheapest option. The mean margin between the best and second-best aircraft widens from 0.023 to 0.087 under the same change; the near-tie rate, which pulls in the opposite direction and is the parameter that inflates a search tree, is controlled separately.
4.5 A planted and provable optimum
To validate that a solver reaches the true optimum rather than a good feasible answer, a bracket is not sufficient; an exact value is required. Repricing supplies one. If for every leg the designated aircraft is priced strictly below every other, then the per-leg minima are attained simultaneously by a single certified-feasible schedule. The sum of the per-leg minima is a valid lower bound on every feasible schedule, since each leg must be flown by some aircraft, and a lower bound that is attained is the optimum. No solver is needed to establish it.
On the medium preset this yields a proven optimum of 5,604,357.5 cost units, exactly equal to its own lower bound, with the schedule attaining it independently re-certified as feasible. The trade-off is unavoidable and should be stated plainly rather than buried: any instance whose optimum coincides with the per-leg minima is also solvable greedily. The mode exists to validate correctness, not to measure performance. The metrics register the change honestly — the cost-signal and near-tie sub-scores fall to their floor and drag the composite index down, which is the scoring system reporting accurately that the instance has become easy.

Difficulty in this problem class is not one-dimensional, so a single density parameter cannot express it. The metrics are grouped into four families, each answering a different question about an instance, and combined only at the very end.
Family Representative measures What it detects
A. Decision freedom Connection density; cross-tail ratio; assignment entropy in bits; mean candidate aircraft per leg Whether the instance offers any routing choice at all, or is effectively pre-solved
B. Constraint tightness Peak and mean station-day capacity utilisation; due-date slack distribution and its coefficient of variation; active check types Whether capacity and maintenance limits actually bind, or merely appear in the model
C. Relaxation hardness Share of legs where the planted aircraft is also cheapest; mean top-two cost margin; near-tie rate; cost spread Whether the objective points at the answer, and whether the relaxation is likely to be fractional
D. Structure and symmetry Eulerian balance and decomposability per day; distinct overnight stations per aircraft; station degree entropy; algebraic connectivity; interchangeable-aircraft groups Whether the network is genuinely rearrangeable, and how much of any observed difficulty is mere symmetry
The composite index normalises each sub-score against a reference range and takes their geometric mean. Choosing the geometric rather than the arithmetic mean is deliberate: a single collapsed dimension drives the whole index down instead of being averaged away by strong scores elsewhere. That is precisely the failure mode the baseline configuration exhibits — instances that are large, numerically well-behaved and superficially realistic, but that offer almost no routing choice. An arithmetic mean would score them respectably.
Every metric is computed from an instance and a certificate, and from nothing else. Two consequences are worth noting. Properties the generator believes it has produced are re-derived from the published data, so a bug in the generator cannot hide behind its own bookkeeping. And because the inputs are just an instance and a schedule, the same metrics can be computed on instances produced by any other method, including sub-sampled ones, which makes the two families of instances directly comparable on a common scale.

Three readings deserve comment. Connection density rises roughly thirteen-fold once the Eulerian decomposition replaces the out-and-back pattern, and the cross-tail ratio — the share of feasible connections joining legs flown by different aircraft — rises from 0.007 to 0.926. In the baseline, virtually every feasible connection is internal to a single aircraft's route, which is another way of saying the routing decision has already been made.
The leg count falls in the final row, from 7,200 to 5,448, and that is not a regression. With all four check types active, aircraft spend materially more of the horizon on the ground, so fewer legs fit into the same window. Density and leg count must therefore be read together; the final configuration has fewer legs but far more structure per leg, and it is the only one in which station capacity binds.
None of these movements is visible to a density parameter, and none of them is available to a procedure that samples from a fixed timetable, because the routing structure of a sub-sampled instance is a property of the source data rather than a parameter of the sampler. Conversely, the reference procedure buys something synthesis cannot: block time distributions, station geography and cost data that are real rather than modelled. The honest conclusion is that the two methods are complementary, and a serious benchmark suite would draw on both. Since the metrics defined here require only an instance and a certificate, they can be applied to sub-sampled instances to establish where on this scale real-timetable extracts actually sit.
