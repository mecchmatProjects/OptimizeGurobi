This chapter develops the ground-routing component of the thesis. The model is
based on the compact tail-assignment formulation of
\citet{khaled2018compact}, but maintenance decisions are deliberately excluded
until Chapter~\ref{ch:maintenance}. This separation isolates the physical
conditions that make a flight assignment executable and ensures that the
maintenance extension is built on valid aircraft trajectories.

The term \emph{compact} refers to the variable space. Route-based models use
variables for complete rotations, and arc-flow models use variables for
feasible flight connections. The formulation below instead uses one binary
variable for each flight--aircraft pair. It therefore has
$O(|\F||\Pc|)$ binary variables and can be submitted directly to a MILP
solver. Airport continuity and timing are recovered through cumulative balance
inequalities. This chapter derives those inequalities, identifies their
equal-time departure limitation, and presents the corrected ground model used
throughout the thesis.

\section{Scope and modelling assumptions}
\label{sec:basic}

The model is defined over a finite planning horizon using absolute times from
the start of that horizon. A flight on day two consequently has larger time
values than flights on day one. The following assumptions delimit the ground
model:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item every published flight leg must be covered exactly once;
    \item each aircraft has one known initial airport;
    \item an aircraft moves between airports only through an assigned flight, so
    any permitted ferry movement must be included explicitly;
    \item departure and arrival times are fixed, and consecutive flights require
    at least the minimum ground turn time;
    \item forbidden flight--aircraft pairs are removed or fixed to zero; and
    \item maintenance and hangar decisions are deferred to
    Chapters~\ref{ch:maintenance} and~\ref{ch:hierarchy}.
\end{enumerate}

The model is non-cyclic: an aircraft need not return to its initial airport by
the end of the horizon. Its terminal position may be passed to the next planning
period. Terminal-balance constraints can impose cyclicity, but this is an
additional planning policy and may make a finite-horizon instance infeasible.

\section{Sets, parameters, and cumulative flight sets}

Let $\F=\{1,\ldots,n_f\}$ be the set of flight legs,
$\Pc=\{1,\ldots,n_p\}$ the set of individual aircraft, and
$\A=\{1,\ldots,n_k\}$ the set of airports. For flight $i\in\F$, let
$a_i^{\uparrow}$ and $a_i^{\downarrow}$ be its departure and arrival airports,
and let $t_i^{\uparrow}$ and $t_i^{\downarrow}$ be its departure and arrival
times. The arrow notation follows the flight event: $\uparrow$ denotes a
departure and $\downarrow$ an arrival. Parameter $a_j^0$ is the initial airport
of aircraft $j$, $\dt>0$ is the minimum turn time, and $c_{ij}$ is the cost of
assigning flight $i$ to aircraft $j$.

For airport $k\in\A$ and threshold $\theta$, define
\begin{align}
    \F_k^{\downarrow}(\theta)
    &=\{i\in\F:a_i^{\downarrow}=k,\;
            t_i^{\downarrow}\leq\theta-\dt\},
            \label{eq:ground_arrival_set}\\
    \F_k^{\uparrow}(\theta)
    &=\{i\in\F:a_i^{\uparrow}=k,\;
            t_i^{\uparrow}<\theta\}.
            \label{eq:ground_departure_set}
\end{align}
The arrival set contains flights that have reached $k$ early enough to complete
the turn before $\theta$. The departure set contains flights that have left $k$
strictly before $\theta$. The strict inequality is central to the formulation
and to the counterexample in Section~\ref{sec:ground_counterexample}.

The cumulative inventory of aircraft $j$ at airport $k$ is
\begin{equation}
    B_{jk}(\theta)=
    \sum_{i\in\F_k^{\downarrow}(\theta)}x_{ij}
    -\sum_{i\in\F_k^{\uparrow}(\theta)}x_{ij}.
    \label{eq:ground_inventory}
\end{equation}
At a non-initial airport, positive inventory can arise only from an earlier
arrival. At $a_j^0$, one additional unit represents the initial aircraft.

\section{Compact mixed-integer formulation}

The binary assignment variable is
\begin{equation}
    x_{ij}=
    \begin{cases}
        1, & \text{if flight $i$ is operated by aircraft $j$,}\\
        0, & \text{otherwise,}
    \end{cases}
    \qquad i\in\F,\;j\in\Pc.
    \label{eq:ground_assignment_variable}
\end{equation}
The objective minimises total assignment cost:
\begin{equation}
    \min\quad\sum_{i\in\F}\sum_{j\in\Pc}c_{ij}x_{ij}.
    \label{eq:ground_objective}
\end{equation}
The coefficients may represent operating cost or assignment preference. A
forbidden pair must be removed or fixed to zero because a large cost alone does
not make an assignment infeasible.

\subsection{C1: flight coverage}

Each flight is assigned to exactly one aircraft:
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{j\in\Pc}x_{ij}=1,
    \qquad\forall i\in\F.
    \label{eq:ground_c1}
\end{equation}
This prevents both uncovered flights and duplicate coverage, but does not alone
make the flights assigned to an aircraft a connected route.

\subsection{C2: continuity at non-initial airports}

For flight $i$ departing from $k\neq a_j^0$, aircraft $j$ must have reached
$k$ early enough and must not have consumed that arrival in an earlier
departure:
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{i'\in\F_k^{\downarrow}(t_i^{\uparrow})}x_{i'j}
    -\sum_{i'\in\F_k^{\uparrow}(t_i^{\uparrow})}x_{i'j}
    \geq x_{ij},
    \quad
    \substack{\forall j\in\Pc,\;k\in\A\setminus\{a_j^0\},\\
    \forall i\in\F:a_i^{\uparrow}=k.}
    \label{eq:ground_c2}
\end{equation}
If $x_{ij}=1$, this is equivalent to
$B_{jk}(t_i^{\uparrow})\geq1$. Because only arrivals with
$t_{i'}^{\downarrow}+\dt\leq t_i^{\uparrow}$ are counted, C2 also embeds the
minimum turn-time requirement.

\subsection{C3: continuity at the initial airport}

At $k=a_j^0$, aircraft $j$ is available once at the start of the horizon, so
the right-hand side is shifted by one:
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{i'\in\F_k^{\downarrow}(t_i^{\uparrow})}x_{i'j}
    -\sum_{i'\in\F_k^{\uparrow}(t_i^{\uparrow})}x_{i'j}
    \geq x_{ij}-1,
    \quad
    \substack{\forall j\in\Pc,\;k=a_j^0,\\
    \forall i\in\F:a_i^{\uparrow}=k.}
    \label{eq:ground_c3}
\end{equation}
The first departure can use the initial aircraft. Every later departure from
the initial airport must be supported by a return arrival after accounting for
earlier departures.

Finally,
\begin{equation}
    x_{ij}\in\{0,1\},
    \qquad\forall i\in\F,\;j\in\Pc.
    \label{eq:ground_integrality}
\end{equation}

\section{Interpretation and validity of the balances}

Constraints~\eqref{eq:ground_c2}--\eqref{eq:ground_c3} replace explicit
connection variables with airport inventories. A turn-feasible arrival adds one
unit to~\eqref{eq:ground_inventory}; an earlier departure subtracts one unit.
For distinct departure events, the inequalities implement conservation: an
aircraft cannot depart a non-initial airport before arriving, and it cannot
reuse an arrival after that inventory unit has been consumed.

The model does not select a predecessor flight explicitly. If several feasible
arrivals precede a departure, the cumulative inequality certifies that one
aircraft unit is present without naming the connection. This aggregation gives
the model its small variable count, but makes correctness depend on event order.

\begin{proposition}[Validity under distinct departure events]
    \label{prop:ground_distinct_validity}
    If no two flights depart from the same airport at the same time, every
    integral solution of
    \eqref{eq:ground_c1}--\eqref{eq:ground_integrality} satisfies coverage,
    initial-position feasibility, airport continuity, and minimum turn time.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    Coverage follows from~\eqref{eq:ground_c1}. Fix aircraft $j$ and process its
    assigned departures in increasing time order. At a non-initial airport,
    \eqref{eq:ground_c2} requires at least one more turn-feasible assigned arrival
    than earlier assigned departures. The current departure is therefore
    supported by an unused arrival at the same airport. At the initial airport,
    \eqref{eq:ground_c3} gives the same argument with one initial unit. Membership
    in $\F_k^{\downarrow}(t_i^{\uparrow})$ guarantees the turn time. Distinct
    departure times ensure that every earlier departure is already subtracted, so
    one inventory unit cannot support two departures.
\end{proof}

\section{Equal-time departure counterexample}
\label{sec:ground_counterexample}

The distinct-event condition is not guaranteed by a timetable. Because
\eqref{eq:ground_departure_set} uses a strict inequality, two flights leaving
the same airport at the same time do not appear in each other's departure sets.
Both can consume the same aircraft inventory.

\begin{example}[Algebraically feasible but physically impossible]
    \label{ex:ground_simultaneous_departure}
    Let one aircraft $j$ start at B and let $\dt=30$ minutes. Consider
    \begin{align*}
        i_0 &: \text{B}\rightarrow\text{A},
            &t_{i_0}^{\uparrow}=500,&&t_{i_0}^{\downarrow}=550,\\
        i_1 &: \text{A}\rightarrow\text{B},
            &t_{i_1}^{\uparrow}=600,&&t_{i_1}^{\downarrow}=700,\\
        i_2 &: \text{A}\rightarrow\text{C},
            &t_{i_2}^{\uparrow}=600,&&t_{i_2}^{\downarrow}=700.
    \end{align*}
    Set $x_{i_0j}=x_{i_1j}=x_{i_2j}=1$. Flight $i_0$ uses the initial unit at B.
    For $i_1$ at A,
    \begin{equation*}
        \F_{\mathrm A}^{\downarrow}(600)=\{i_0\},\qquad
        \F_{\mathrm A}^{\uparrow}(600)=\varnothing,
    \end{equation*}
    because $i_0$ departs from B and $i_2$ departs at 600, not strictly before
    600. Thus C2 gives $1-0\geq1$. The calculation for $i_2$ is identical because
    $i_1$ is also excluded from its strict departure set. All compact constraints
    are satisfied, although one aircraft cannot operate $i_1$ and $i_2$ at the
    same time.
\end{example}

The defect is structural rather than cost-dependent. Maintenance constraints
also cannot repair duplicated use of an aircraft. Physical non-overlap must be
enforced in the ground model.

\section{Conflict graph and corrected feasibility model}

Associate with flight $i$ the occupied interval
\begin{equation}
    I_i=[t_i^{\uparrow},t_i^{\downarrow}+\dt).
    \label{eq:ground_occupied_interval}
\end{equation}
The half-open convention allows a following flight to depart exactly when the
required turn ends. Define the set of unordered conflicting pairs
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal O=\bigl\{\{i_1,i_2\}\subseteq\F:
    I_{i_1}\cap I_{i_2}\neq\varnothing\bigr\}.
    \label{eq:ground_overlap_set}
\end{equation}
For every conflict and aircraft, impose
\begin{equation}
    x_{i_1j}+x_{i_2j}\leq1,
    \qquad
    \forall\{i_1,i_2\}\in\mathcal O,\;\forall j\in\Pc.
    \label{eq:ground_overlap}
\end{equation}
These constraints exclude simultaneous flights, overlapping legs, and
connections with insufficient turn time. Some rows duplicate restrictions
already implied by C2--C3, but the complete conflict set gives a direct and
auditable statement of single-aircraft non-overlap.

\begin{proposition}[Physical feasibility of the corrected model]
    \label{prop:ground_corrected_validity}
    Under this chapter's assumptions, every integral solution satisfying
    \eqref{eq:ground_c1}--\eqref{eq:ground_integrality} and
    \eqref{eq:ground_overlap} yields one temporally ordered,
    airport-continuous sequence for each aircraft.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    Fix aircraft $j$. Constraint~\eqref{eq:ground_overlap} makes the occupied
    intervals of its assigned flights pairwise disjoint, giving an unambiguous
    temporal order with the required turn separation. Process that order. At a
    non-initial airport, C2 requires an unused earlier arrival at the departure
    airport. At the initial airport, C3 supplies one initial unit and thereafter
    requires return arrivals. Since assigned occupied intervals do not overlap,
    an earlier aircraft movement cannot support concurrent continuations. Every
    departure is therefore connected to the initial position or to a preceding
    turn-feasible arrival at the same airport.
\end{proof}

The corrected model comprises objective~\eqref{eq:ground_objective},
C1--C3, C4$'$, and integrality. It changes neither the assignment variables nor
the objective; it removes assignments that cannot represent a physical route.

\section{Model size and computational implications}

The original model has $n_fn_p$ binary variables, $n_f$ coverage equations,
and at most one continuity row per flight--aircraft pair. Its row count is
$O(n_fn_p)$. This compares favourably with the $O(n_f^2n_p)$
connection-variable structure of \citet{levin1971scheduling};
\citet{khaled2018compact} also report fewer variables and constraints than the
compared time-space formulation on their instances.

The correction adds $|\mathcal O|n_p$ inequalities, producing
\begin{equation}
    O\bigl(n_fn_p+|\mathcal O|n_p\bigr)
    \label{eq:ground_model_size}
\end{equation}
rows. In the worst case $|\mathcal O|=O(n_f^2)$, so the corrected row count is
not universally linear. In realistic timetables, a flight conflicts only with
flights whose occupied intervals intersect its own; a sweep over flights ordered
by departure time generates these pairs efficiently. The model remains compact
in its binary-variable space.

The pairwise inequalities may also strengthen the LP relaxation. Clique
inequalities could be generated for sets of mutually overlapping flights, but
the pairwise form is retained because it is transparent, easy to validate, and
sufficient for integral physical feasibility.

\section{Illustrative non-cyclic assignment}

Table~\ref{tab:ground_example_timetable} reproduces the structure of the
six-flight example in \citet{khaled2018compact}. The turn time is 30 minutes;
aircraft 1 starts at A and aircraft 2 at B.

    \centering
    \small
    \caption{Six-flight timetable for the illustrative ground assignment.}
    \label{tab:ground_example_timetable}
    \begin{tabular}{ccccc}
        \toprule
        Flight & Departure & Arrival & Departure time & Arrival time \\
        \midrule
        1 & A & B & 09:00 & 11:00 \\
        2 & B & A & 11:30 & 13:30 \\
        3 & A & B & 14:00 & 16:00 \\
        4 & B & A & 09:30 & 11:30 \\
        5 & A & B & 12:00 & 14:00 \\
        6 & B & A & 14:30 & 16:30 \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

One feasible assignment is
\begin{align*}
    \text{aircraft 1: }&1\rightarrow2\rightarrow3,\\
    \text{aircraft 2: }&4\rightarrow5\rightarrow6.
\end{align*}
Every connection preserves airport continuity and uses exactly the 30-minute
turn. Aircraft 1 ends at B and aircraft 2 at A, so the assignment is feasible
for the non-cyclic horizon. Requiring both aircraft to return to their initial
airports would reject it, illustrating that cyclicity is a terminal policy and
not a requirement for physical feasibility within the horizon.

\section{Chapter summary}

The compact model obtains a small assignment-variable space by replacing
explicit connections with cumulative airport balances. Those balances encode
initial position, continuity, and turn time when departures are strictly
ordered. Equal-time departures expose a missing case because two flights can
consume the same inventory. The conflict inequalities
\eqref{eq:ground_overlap} close this gap and establish the physically executable
domain used by the maintenance model in Chapter~\ref{ch:maintenance}.
